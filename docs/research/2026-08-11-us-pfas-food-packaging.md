# US State Bans on Intentionally Added PFAS in Food Packaging / Food Contact Materials

**Research pass:** 2026-08-11 · **Method:** primary sources (statute text, agency pages) wherever
possible. The WebSearch budget was exhausted early; the remainder was done by direct retrieval of statute
and agency URLs. · **Status:** point-in-time snapshot, not maintained.

> **How to use this.** Anything not confirmed from a primary source is marked **unverified** rather than
> guessed. **Not yet encoded** into `app/scoring/producer_attribution.py` — `pfas` is not currently in
> the `Regime` vocabulary at all. This brief is the source for adding it.
>
> **Date sensitivity:** Maine's 25 May 2026 date has passed as of this research date, so Maine is now
> **in force**, not future-dated — a distinction most trade coverage written in 2025 gets wrong.

## 2. The distinctions that actually matter operationally

**Plant-fiber-only vs. all-materials.** WA, CA, NY, MD, CO cover only paper/paperboard/plant-fiber
packaging. **MN, CT, RI, VT, OR, NM cover *any* direct-food-contact package regardless of substrate** —
so plastic-lined cups, films, and lids are in scope there, not just fiber.

**Only one state has a numeric threshold.** California is the outlier: **≥ 100 ppm total organic
fluorine** is a violation *even with no intentional addition* (HSC §109000(a)(3)). Every other state on
this list is intentional-addition-only, meaning the compliance artifact is a supplier attestation, not a
lab result. California is the state where recycled fiber feedstock or supply-chain carryover can put you
over the line without anyone having added PFAS. **Do not assume a 100 ppm number applies anywhere else.**

**Maine is the only state with a revenue-based exemption:** food/beverage manufacturers with **< $1,000,000,000**
in total annual national food and beverage sales are exempt from the food-package prohibition (32 M.R.S. §1733(3-C)).

### The QSR-specific certificate burden

In most states the certificate obligation lands on the *manufacturer* and is triggered by an agency
request. Two exceptions matter for restaurant operators:

- **New York is the sharpest.** ECL 37-0211 puts the duty on the **person selling food in the packaging**
  — i.e. the restaurant — to obtain signed written certification from each supplier and keep it on site
  for DEC inspection. **That is a per-SKU document-retention program, not a purchasing decision.**
- **Maryland** requires manufacturers/distributors to **establish** a certificate as a standing obligation
  (not merely on request), producible to MDE within 30 days.
- **Connecticut** (§22a-255k) requires the certificate to be furnished to the **purchaser** on request —
  so a QSR can compel it, turnaround 60 days (30 if DEEP suspects a violation).
- **Washington** requires certificates on file for the life of the packaging **plus three years** after
  last sale/distribution, with Ecology-published templates by phase.
- **California AB 347** adds a registration + statement-of-compliance filing with DTSC by **July 1, 2029**,
  with DTSC empowered to demand analytical test results. First-violation administrative penalty floor is
  **$10,000**.

> ⚠️ **A citation trap worth carrying into the codebase.** Penalties vary by three orders of magnitude —
> Maine's ≤$100 per package, Oregon's ≤$500/day, New Mexico's ≤$15,000 plus ≤$25,000/day, California's
> $10,000 floor. Several state penalty provisions could not be tied definitively to the food-packaging
> subtitle (MD, CO, RI, NY) and are marked unverified rather than transplanted. **Both the Colorado
> $5,000/$10,000 and the Maryland $500/$1,000 figures that circulate in trade coverage sit in the
> *firefighting-foam* and *rug-and-carpet* parts of those acts, not the packaging parts.**

## 3. Federal angle — FDA

**This is a voluntary market exit, not a ban.** FDA's own language:

> "In February 2024, the FDA announced that substances containing PFAS used as grease-proofing agents on
> paper and paperboard for food contact use are **no longer being sold by manufacturers into the U.S.
> market**." — [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/process-contaminants-food/authorized-uses-pfas-food-contact-applications)

The mechanism, per FDA on the same page:

> "As a result, the FDA worked with industry to reach **voluntary market phase-out agreements** for all
> grease-proofers that contain 6:2 FTOH… In addition, manufacturers of the remaining PFAS containing
> grease-proofers authorized for food-contact use have **voluntarily stopped selling them for non-safety
> reasons**."

The trigger was FDA's spring 2020 review of 6:2 fluorotelomer alcohol migration data. FDA notes
grease-proofers were the **only** one of the four authorized PFAS food-contact categories where dietary
exposure presented a potential safety concern — because grease-proofers are applied at low temperatures
that don't drive off migratable short-chain PFAS.

**The follow-through is the closest thing to a prohibition:** in **January 2025** FDA published a Federal
Register notice determining that the **35 food contact notifications (FCNs)** for PFAS grease-proofers on
paper/paperboard are **no longer effective, based on abandonment of these uses**. The authorizations are
gone — but by abandonment, not by a safety-based revocation or rulemaking ban. A new market entrant would
have to file a fresh FCN.

**Practical read for a QSR:** the federal action removed the supply of *newly sold* PFAS grease-proofing
agents, but created **no compliance obligation, no threshold, no certificate requirement and no penalty**.
FDA also cautions that total-fluorine screening "cannot discern between the presence of PFAS or the
presence of other non-concerning fluorine containing substances" — relevant when interpreting a
supplier's TOF number against California's 100 ppm test.

## 4. Cost impact on a QSR — what could and could not be sourced

**No sourced dollar figure was found** for the price delta between PFAS-treated and PFAS-free
grease-resistant packaging (fry cartons, sandwich wraps, molded fiber bowls). Washington Ecology's
alternatives-assessment report (Publication 21-04-007), which contains the cost analysis, was not
retrievable. **No number was invented.**

The one cost fact that *is* sourced and legally load-bearing:

- Both **Washington** and **Maine** may only impose the prohibition after the agency affirmatively finds
  that safer alternatives are **"readily available in sufficient quantity and at a comparable cost, and
  perform as well as or better than PFAS"** in that specific application
  ([RCW 70A.222.070(3)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=70A.222.070);
  [32 M.R.S. §1733(3-B)(A)](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/32/title32sec1733.html)). Washington
  made that finding category by category, which is exactly why the ban arrived in two phases — the fiber
  categories where alternatives were not yet cost-comparable were held back.
- **Vermont** applies the same "comparable cost" standard to bisphenols, and imposes a **two-year lead
  time** after rule adoption before any such prohibition takes effect (9 V.S.A. §2494x(b)).

In other words: the statutory design in the phased states presumes near-cost-parity as a precondition, and
the phase structure is the state's own admission of where parity had not yet arrived. **That is the
defensible framing.** Anyone quoting a specific "PFAS-free costs X% more" figure should be asked for the
underlying source.

## 5. Caveats on completeness

- **Hawaii, New Jersey, Illinois, Virginia**: no enacted food-packaging PFAS ban confirmed from primary
  sources. Hawaii is listed in several secondary trackers, but capitol.hawaii.gov blocked automated
  retrieval (403) and Hawaii DOH's PFAS page describes PFAS in food packaging only as an exposure pathway.
  Treat all four as **unverified**, not as confirmed negatives.
- **Colorado successor legislation (HB24-1161)** was not verified — the HB22-1345 provision at C.R.S.
  §25-15-604(1)(c) is confirmed in force, but any subsequent amendment is **unverified**.
- The WebSearch budget was exhausted early, which is why coverage of any 2025–2026 additions beyond New
  Mexico is thin. **New Mexico HB 212 (2025) is the significant confirmed addition**, future-dated to
  1 Jan 2027 for both food packaging and cookware.
